# 6. Conclusion

This study evaluated satellite cyber-response and trusted-recovery policies in a controlled software-in-the-loop environment using a frozen 24-cell design and 720 VALID repeated observations. The experiment preserved separate security, mission, recovery, command-availability, and evidence outcomes rather than optimizing a single composite performance score.

The findings do not support a universal mission-aware-policy advantage. Mission-state dependence was not demonstrated on the predeclared P1 primary outcomes. One modeled missed-contact window, however, produced an approximately 10-s increase in containment, verified-recovery, and ground/spacecraft divergence timing for the ground-authorized P6 path while the corresponding P7 contact contrasts were approximately zero. Reduced policy-visible evidence also materially changed P7 behavior: in the compromised-update block, the deterministic rule-based selector moved from complete trusted recovery under full evidence to complete recovery failure under T1 field omission/reduction, while fixed P5 retained trusted recovery. Across the condition-matched P5 comparisons, P7 could be beneficial, equivalent, mixed, or dominated depending on the scenario.

These results support a bounded cybersecurity and systems-engineering conclusion. Satellite cyber-response mechanisms should be evaluated with explicit authorization, contact, evidence, and trusted-recovery assumptions and with separate mission/security outcomes. Context-sensitive rule-based selection can avoid weak baselines or authorization delays, but it can also select a worse mission/recovery trade-off when required policy-visible evidence is unavailable. Conversely, fixed recovery mechanisms can outperform contextual selection in some conditions.

The main contribution is therefore a reproducible **post-detection response/recovery comparison method** and integrity-frozen outcome record rather than a new intrusion detector, safe-mode concept, general autonomy theory, or operational flight-recovery architecture. The study also illustrates the value of retaining null findings, negative policy comparisons, right-censored recovery outcomes, invalid-attempt provenance, and versioned execution history in cyber-resilience experiments.

Future work should be conducted as separately frozen studies rather than appended to the present 720-observation population. A security/dependability extension should test distinct evidence-failure mechanisms, multiple contact regimes, fault/attack ambiguity, selector ablation, stronger non-adaptive baselines, and formal response/recovery invariants. A later aerospace validation phase should add modeled orbital/access schedules, flight-like resource measurements, richer mission phases, and an RF-free hardware-in-the-loop subset. Human-in-the-loop authorization and operational decision latency should be studied separately under appropriate human-subject and operational approvals. Any extension toward RF, proprietary telemetry, or operational assets would require a new safety/legal review.

Within its software-in-the-loop boundary, the central research question is not simply whether a spacecraft can respond autonomously to a cyber event. The more informative question is whether a given response can contain the modeled threat, preserve prioritized mission service, maintain command availability, and return to an evidence-supported trusted state under the information and contact constraints available to the response mechanism.
