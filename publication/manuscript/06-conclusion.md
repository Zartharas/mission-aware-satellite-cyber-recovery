# 6. Conclusion

This study evaluated satellite cyber-response and trusted-recovery policies in a controlled software-in-the-loop environment using a frozen 24-cell design and 720 VALID repeated observations. The experiment was structured to preserve separate security, mission, recovery, command-availability, and evidence outcomes rather than optimize a single composite performance score.

The findings do not support a universal mission-aware-policy advantage. Mission-state dependence was not demonstrated on the predeclared P1 primary outcomes. One modeled missed-contact window, however, produced an approximately 10-s increase in containment, verified-recovery, and ground/spacecraft divergence timing for the ground-authorized P6 path while the corresponding P7 contact contrasts were approximately zero. Degraded policy-visible evidence also materially changed P7 behavior: in the compromised-update block, P7 moved from complete trusted recovery under full evidence to complete recovery failure under degraded evidence, while fixed P5 retained trusted recovery. Across the condition-matched P5 comparisons, P7 could be beneficial, equivalent, mixed, or dominated depending on the scenario.

These results support a bounded systems-engineering conclusion. Satellite cyber-response mechanisms should be evaluated with explicit contact and evidence assumptions, trusted-recovery criteria, and separate mission/security outcomes. Context-sensitive automation can avoid weak baselines or authorization delays, but it can also select a worse mission/recovery trade-off when its policy-visible evidence is degraded. Conversely, fixed recovery mechanisms can outperform an adaptive selector in some conditions.

The main contribution is therefore a reproducible comparative evaluation method and integrity-frozen outcome record rather than a new safe-mode concept, a new general autonomy theory, or an operational flight-recovery architecture. The study also illustrates the value of retaining null findings, negative policy comparisons, right-censored recovery outcomes, invalid-attempt provenance, and versioned execution history in cyber-resilience experiments.

Future work should test whether these conditional relationships persist under richer spacecraft dynamics, additional mission phases and resource constraints, more realistic contact schedules, broader adversary capabilities, hardware- or processor-in-the-loop execution, and independently designed response policies. Human-in-the-loop authorization and operational decision latency should be studied separately under appropriate human-subject and operational approvals. Any extension toward RF, flight-like hardware, proprietary telemetry, or operational assets would require a new safety/legal review and is outside the authorization of the present work.

Within its software-in-the-loop boundary, the experiment demonstrates that the central research question is not simply whether a spacecraft can respond autonomously to a cyber event. The more informative question is whether a given response can contain the modeled threat, preserve prioritized mission service, maintain command availability, and return to an evidence-supported trusted state under the specific information and contact constraints available at the time.
