# 1. Introduction

Spacecraft cybersecurity is increasingly treated as a mission-assurance problem rather than only a communications or information-technology problem. A cyber response that blocks an unauthorized command may also interrupt a time-sensitive mission function; a containment action that preserves the system may reduce observability; and a recovery action that restores nominal behavior may still leave uncertainty about software integrity, authorization state, configuration provenance, or the trustworthiness of the evidence used to justify recovery. These tensions are amplified when ground contact is intermittent and the evidence available to a response mechanism is incomplete or adversarially influenced [@chunawala2026satelliteir; @nist800160v2r1].

Existing research provides strong foundations for these concerns. Mission Aware cybersecurity links unacceptable mission losses to system functions, architecture, and cyberattack evidence [@bakirtzis2026missionaware]. Spacecraft fault detection, isolation, and recovery research provides mature concepts for state-aware fault response, safing, and restoration [@wanninger2025fdir; @thangavel2024trusted; @sarri2026juice]. Cyber-resilience engineering emphasizes the ability to anticipate, withstand, recover from, and adapt to cyber adversity while reducing mission risk [@nist800160v2r1]. SPARTA and related space-security guidance define cyber-safe and recovery concepts [@sparta_cybersafe], while satellite cybersecurity testbeds and telemetry datasets increasingly support repeatable attack and detection research [@geletko2019nos3; @idan2025aegissat; @chan2026hades; @cucdid_2026]. Recent Computers & Security papers also demonstrate venue adjacency through satellite intrusion detection, SatCom risk analysis, space-organization attack-surface measurement, and cyber-physical security testbeds [@driouch2024cansatids; @casaril2024satcom; @casaril2026attack_surface; @dambrosio2025scass]. These areas establish the relevance of mission context, autonomy, recovery, and satellite cyber experimentation; none is claimed here as a new concept.

A narrower empirical problem remains comparatively under-studied: what happens after a cyber-relevant condition has been established and alternative response/recovery policies must act under mission, contact, and evidence constraints? Detection-focused studies answer an upstream question. The work reported here instead compares post-detection behavior while keeping security containment, mission continuity, command availability, safety constraints, and trusted recovery separate rather than collapsing them into one score.

The journal article reports **two separately frozen software-in-the-loop studies**. Study 1 asks how fixed, ground-authorized, recovery, and deterministic rule-based mission-aware response policies behave across selected cyber events, mission states, evidence omission/reduction, and one modeled missed-contact condition. Its frozen population contains 720 VALID observations from 24 cells and 30 reproducible seed blocks; nine INVALID attempts are retained as provenance but excluded from statistical membership under predeclared validity rules. Study 1 remains unchanged by the later work.

Study 2 is a prospectively specified security/dependability extension with an independently frozen population of 3,872 VALID observations across 85 cells and zero INVALID attempts. It broadens the evidence model to current, omitted, stale/replayed, contradictory, post-signature-manipulated, and bounded producer-compromise conditions; expands contact modeling to K0–K3 ordered outage conditions plus a separate K4 intermittent/flapping condition; introduces adversary-budget stress and context ablations; and compares interpretable fail-closed, fail-operational, risk-threshold, and evidence-aware policies. The two populations are never pooled for statistical inference.

Trusted recovery is not declared solely because a simulated system appears operational. Across the research program, recovery is conditioned on policy-visible and/or adjudication evidence appropriate to the frozen design. Study 2 sharpens this distinction by separating **evidence-qualified trusted recovery** from research-only objective adjudication truth. A policy can therefore satisfy a recovery gate on authenticated/current evidence even when a compromised evidence producer has made that evidence false relative to the hidden research truth. The adjudication oracle is never selector input.

The analysis deliberately avoids a universal policy winner. Study 1 preserves separate security, mission, recovery, and command-availability outcomes and uses condition-specific Pareto comparisons for its multi-objective question. Study 2 likewise prohibits a weighted global policy score or global policy rank. Primary Block-A effects are reported with paired effect estimates and confidence intervals, while prespecified secondary contrast families use Holm adjustment within family and endpoint. Secondary 32-seed blocks are treated as estimation/sensitivity evidence rather than as small-effect confirmatory tests.

The empirical record is intentionally non-uniform. In Study 1, mission-state dependence was not demonstrated on predeclared primary outcomes; one modeled missed-contact window strongly delayed the ground-authorized path; controlled omission/reduction of policy-visible evidence materially changed the deterministic selector's recovery and action-selection pathways; and the mission-aware policy showed benefit, equivalence, mixed performance, and disadvantage depending on condition. In Study 2, bounded producer compromise demonstrated that authenticated/current policy-visible evidence and evidence-qualified recovery are not equivalent to objective correctness; contact/authorization experiments distinguished an evidence-aware wait/recover behavior from an initially permissive fail-closed baseline under non-immediate contact; context ablations exposed specific dependencies; and stronger adversary-budget profiles revealed residual unauthorized-state risk without supporting a global policy ranking.

One Study-2 result requires an explicit interpretation boundary. The Block-C BENIGN/ADVERSARIAL cause label does not change hidden truth or generated policy-visible evidence within each frozen ambiguity family. Its 54 zero contrasts are therefore a **structural label-invariance control**, not empirical evidence that the evaluated policies can or cannot discriminate genuinely different benign and adversarial causal mechanisms. This limitation is retained in the journal claims rather than promoted into a stronger result.

The work makes five bounded contributions. First, it provides a reproducible satellite-specific method for comparing post-detection cyber-response and trusted-recovery policies under controlled mission, contact, and evidence conditions. Second, it operationalizes recovery as an evidence-qualified state rather than nominal restoration alone. Third, it separates evidence authenticity/currentness from objective correctness under a bounded producer-compromise model. Fourth, it reports condition-specific and adverse outcomes without a weighted global score or universal policy ranking. Fifth, it preserves a reproducible evidence chain across both studies, including frozen cells and seeds, retained invalid-attempt provenance where applicable, cryptographic evidence identities, independently reproduced Study-2 statistics, and durable result artifacts.

The research remains bounded to controlled software-in-the-loop experimentation using NOS3/Fortytwo and cFS-based components [@nasa_nos3; @nasa_cfs]. It does not access an operational spacecraft or ground station, transmit or interfere with RF, use operational credentials, measure real operator response time, establish flight latency, implement flight certification, or demonstrate a production autonomous recovery architecture. Study-2 time values are deterministic logical SIL time rather than real-link or wall-clock latency.

The remainder of the paper summarizes the theoretical and empirical context, defines the cybersecurity threat and trust boundaries, describes the two frozen experimental designs and analyses, reports their results separately, and then synthesizes what the combined evidence does—and does not—support for satellite cyber-response and trusted-recovery engineering.
