# 1. Introduction

Spacecraft cybersecurity is increasingly treated as a mission-assurance problem rather than only a communications or information-technology problem. A cyber response that blocks an unauthorized command may also interrupt a time-sensitive mission function; a containment action that preserves the system may reduce observability; and a recovery action that restores nominal behavior may still leave uncertainty about software integrity, authorization state, or configuration provenance. These tensions are amplified when ground contact is intermittent and the evidence available to a response mechanism is incomplete or stale.

Existing research provides strong foundations for these concerns. Mission Aware cybersecurity links unacceptable mission losses to system functions, architecture, and cyberattack evidence. Spacecraft fault detection, isolation, and recovery (FDIR) research provides mature concepts for state-aware fault response, safing, and restoration. Cyber-resilience engineering emphasizes the ability to anticipate, withstand, recover from, and adapt to cyber adversity while reducing mission risk. SPARTA and related space-security guidance define cyber-safe and recovery concepts, while satellite cybersecurity testbeds and telemetry datasets increasingly support repeatable attack and detection research. These areas establish the relevance of mission context, autonomy, safe modes, recovery, and satellite cyber experimentation; none of them is claimed here as a new concept.

A narrower empirical problem remains. Much of the available satellite cybersecurity literature concentrates on threat modeling, vulnerability analysis, detection, telemetry anomaly recognition, testbed construction, or individual defensive mechanisms. Conversely, spacecraft FDIR and autonomy studies generally address non-adversarial faults or operational contingencies. The unresolved comparison problem is what happens when multiple cyber-response and recovery policies are evaluated under the same controlled satellite conditions while security containment, mission continuity, command availability, safety constraints, and evidence-qualified trusted recovery are measured separately.

This study addresses that problem with a reproducible software-in-the-loop experiment. The design compares fixed policies, a ground-authorized recovery policy, and a mission-aware selector across frozen synthetic cyber events, spacecraft mission states, policy-visible evidence conditions, and contact availability. Recovery is not declared solely because a simulated system appears operational. `TRUSTED_RECOVERY_CONFIRMED` requires current evidence of approved software/configuration, integrity and authorization, restored command authority and telemetry, state agreement, health, and absence of modeled residual unauthorized state.

The experiment deliberately avoids a single composite performance score. A response that improves one endpoint can worsen another, and an arbitrary weighting could obscure mission-security trade-offs. The primary analysis therefore retains separate outcomes and uses condition-specific Pareto comparisons for the multi-objective policy question. The design also retains unexpected scientific outcomes and invalid infrastructure/measurement attempts rather than silently deleting them.

Five propositions structure the analysis. P1 tests whether policy value and cost differ by mission state. P2 tests whether a modeled missed-contact condition disproportionately delays a ground-authorized policy relative to an autonomous path. P3 tests the role of current evidence in declaring trusted recovery. P4 tests whether degraded policy-visible evidence changes actual response selection and downstream consequences. P5 tests whether mission-aware selection can be Pareto-efficient in some conditions without being a universal winner.

The final campaign produced 720 VALID observations from 24 frozen cells and 30 reproducible campaign seeds. The results are intentionally mixed. P1 was not supported on its predeclared primary outcomes. P2 showed a strong timing effect for the ground-authorized P6 path under modeled contact loss. P3 and P4 showed substantial evidence-dependent differences in P7 recovery and selection pathways. P5 showed supported benefits, equivalence, mixed performance, and supported comparator advantages across different matched conditions. This pattern is important because it constrains the contribution: the study does not demonstrate that mission-aware autonomy is universally superior. Instead, it provides controlled evidence about when contextual response selection helps, when it is equivalent to a simpler policy, and when it can produce a worse mission/recovery trade-off.

The study makes four bounded contributions. First, it provides a satellite-specific comparative method for evaluating cyber-response and trusted-recovery policies under explicitly controlled contact and evidence conditions. Second, it operationalizes trusted recovery as an evidence-qualified terminal state rather than nominal behavioral restoration alone. Third, it reports condition-specific multi-objective outcomes, including negative cases for the mission-aware policy, without replacing the endpoints with a weighted score. Fourth, it preserves a reproducible provenance chain: frozen design cells and seeds, retained invalid attempts, per-run execution-commit identities, cryptographic campaign membership, and a final-commit sensitivity analysis.

The experiment is limited to a controlled NOS3/Fortytwo software-in-the-loop environment. It does not access an operational spacecraft or ground station, transmit RF, measure real operator response time, implement a native flight safe mode, or establish flightworthiness or formal safety certification. The contact window is synthetic/modelled, the mission and event set is bounded, and the conclusions are conditional on the implemented policy and evidence model.

The remainder of the paper summarizes the theoretical and empirical context, describes the frozen experimental design and analysis, reports the proposition-level results, and discusses their implications for evaluating satellite cyber response and trusted recovery.
